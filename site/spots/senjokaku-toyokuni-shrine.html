Senjokaku (Toyokuni Shrine)

Hiroshima

Senjokaku, whose name translates roughly to "Pavilion of a Thousand Mats," was commissioned in 1587 by warlord Toyotomi Hideyoshi as a hall for chanting Buddhist sutras on behalf of fallen soldiers. Hideyoshi died in 1598 before construction was completed, and the building was simply left as it was, without ceiling boards over parts of the structure, front doors, and other finishing details, giving it an unusually raw, exposed timber-frame appearance compared with most Japanese religious buildings.

The hall now functions as Toyokuni Shrine, and despite its unfinished state it is enormous by the standards of traditional Japanese wooden architecture, supported by large pillars and open on most sides to the surrounding air, which keeps it naturally cool and breezy even in summer. Visitors can walk across its wide wooden floor, and numerous large votive paintings and plaques donated over the centuries hang from its exposed beams and walls.

It stands immediately beside the five-story pagoda on the hillside above Itsukushima Shrine, and the two are commonly visited as a pair on the short uphill detour from the shrine's exit.

Inside the hall, the reflection of its large ginkgo tree in the polished wooden floor has become a specifically sought-after photo, best captured on quiet weekdays and especially striking in autumn when the tree's leaves turn a deep yellow.

1-1 Miyajima-cho, Hatsukaichi, Hiroshima Prefecture
