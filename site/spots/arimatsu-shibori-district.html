Arimatsu Shibori District

Nagoya

Arimatsu grew up as a rest stop on the old Tokaido highway connecting Edo and Kyoto, and it built its wealth on shibori, a labor-intensive tie-dye technique that produced textiles prized enough to fund the elaborate merchant houses still standing along the main street today. Unlike many historic districts that have been heavily rebuilt for tourism, much of Arimatsu's streetscape is simply original, with tiled roofs, lattice windows, and storehouse walls dating back generations.

Walking the main street is free, and a small museum near the center explains the shibori dyeing process in more depth for a modest admission; workshops occasionally let visitors try a simplified version of the technique themselves for an additional fee. A relaxed visit takes one to two hours.

Because it's a short train ride outside central Nagoya, Arimatsu sees a fraction of the foot traffic of the city's main sights, and even its one annual festival, which draws considerably larger crowds than a normal day, is worth checking the calendar for if you'd rather avoid or specifically catch it.
