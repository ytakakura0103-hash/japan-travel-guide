Rikugien Garden

Tokyo

Rikugien was laid out in the early Edo period by a senior shogunal official as a strolling garden built around scenes and imagery drawn from classical Japanese and Chinese poetry, with paths designed to reveal a sequence of carefully composed views rather than a single open vista — a garden meant to be read almost like a poem as you walk through it. Among its many trees, one weeping cherry near the entrance has become famous enough on its own to draw crowds independent of the rest of the garden each spring, when its cascading branches are often lit in the evening during a special illumination period.

Outside of cherry blossom season, Rikugien is best known for autumn, when its maples turn a deep red and are likewise illuminated after dark for a period in late November and early December — arguably the garden's single most photographed season. A full walk through the grounds takes about an hour at an easy pace, and admission is charged at the gate.

Because of its fame for both the weeping cherry and autumn illumination, Rikugien gets genuinely crowded during those specific windows, particularly evenings and weekends; a weekday or daytime visit is far calmer, and outside peak seasons the garden is one of the more peaceful strolling gardens in the city.

6-16-3 Honkomagome, Bunkyo City, Tokyo
