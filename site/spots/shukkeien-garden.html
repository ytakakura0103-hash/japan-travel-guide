Shukkeien Garden

Hiroshima

Shukkeien was laid out in 1620 for Asano Nagaakira, the daimyo who took over Hiroshima domain after the Mori clan, with the garden traditionally credited to tea master Ueda Soko. Its design follows the shukkei principle of compressing full-scale scenery, such as mountains, valleys, and coastlines, into a small circuit garden centered on Takuei Pond, which is crossed by an arched stone bridge modeled loosely on a bridge in Hangzhou, China.

Like much of central Hiroshima, the garden was devastated in the 1945 bombing and used afterward as a shelter for displaced residents before being restored and reopened to the public in 1951. Some of its older trees and stone lanterns still show damage from that period, and a few informational panels note this history alongside the garden's Edo-period design.

The garden changes noticeably with the seasons: plum blossoms in late winter, azaleas and wisteria in spring, and maple foliage in autumn are the main draws, with the pond's carp and the surrounding teahouses giving it a slower pace than the nearby castle or art museums. A combined ticket with the adjacent Hiroshima Museum of Art is available at the entrance.

2-11 Kaminoborocho, Naka Ward, Hiroshima, Hiroshima Prefecture
