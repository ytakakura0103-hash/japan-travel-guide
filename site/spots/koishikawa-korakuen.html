Koishikawa Korakuen

Tokyo

Koishikawa Korakuen dates to the early Edo period, making it one of the oldest surviving gardens in Tokyo, and it was designed by the Mito branch of the Tokugawa family with the help of a Chinese Confucian scholar, whose influence shows in miniature recreations of famous Chinese and Japanese landscapes scattered around the garden's large central pond — a scaled-down Mount Lu here, a bridge modeled on a scene from Kyoto there. Walking the garden's paths means moving through a sequence of these references in fairly quick succession, which rewards a slow pace over a rushed one.

The garden sits in an unusual setting today, wedged between the Tokyo Dome complex and a busy stretch of central Tokyo, so its high walls and mature trees do a fairly effective job of blocking out the surrounding city once you're inside. A full walk through the grounds takes about an hour, and admission is charged at the gate, generally at a modest rate typical of Tokyo's historic gardens.

Weekday visits are consistently the quietest, and cherry blossoms in spring followed by maple foliage in autumn are the garden's two seasonal highlights, both drawing larger weekend crowds than the rest of the year. Its location a short walk from Iidabashi Station makes it easy to combine with a visit to Tokyo Daijingu nearby.
